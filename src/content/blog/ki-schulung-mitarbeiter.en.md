> **Short answer:** AI training works when three things come together: content by role instead of one-size-fits-all, practice on real tasks from people's own work instead of demo examples, and reinforcement over weeks (champions, office hours, shared prompts) instead of a one-off event. The one-hour inspiration keynote many companies book generates applause and changes nothing. And incidentally: since February 2025, Article 4 of the EU AI Act requires demonstrable AI literacy from everyone deploying AI systems anyway.

I teach Applied AI and prompt engineering at HdM Stuttgart and have run many corporate training sessions over the past few years - from craft businesses to corporate divisions. This article is the distillation of both: what actually sticks in lecture halls and meeting rooms, and what reliably evaporates.

Last updated: June 30, 2026.

## The uncomfortable starting point

Let's begin with the finding that surprises many executives: **the tool is almost never the bottleneck. The skills are.**

Modern LLMs are astonishingly capable - in the hands of people who know how to work with them. The same models are disappointing in untrained hands: vague prompts produce vague results, hallucinations go unnoticed, and after three frustrating attempts the verdict is "useless for my work." That verdict is actually rational - it describes the experience correctly. Only the cause is misattributed.

I see this every semester in miniature: students who have used ChatGPT for years suddenly get results that previously seemed "impossible" after two weeks of structured work on [prompting fundamentals](/en/blog/prompt-engineering-fundamentals). For working professionals the jump is often bigger still, because their tasks are more specific.

## Why the usual formats fail

Three formats I get asked for again and again - and why I advise against two of them:

**The inspiration keynote (60-90 minutes, large audience).** Creates momentum that lasts about a week. After that everything is as before, because nobody managed the transfer into their own work. Fine as the kickoff of a larger initiative - as *the* training measure, money out the window.

**The e-learning course for everyone.** Scales beautifully, gets ignored beautifully. Completion rates beyond padded statistics are sobering, and even those who click through never practice on their own tasks. E-learning works as a reference and for compliance basics - not for behavior change.

**The hands-on workshop with people's own tasks.** The format that works - when participants work on *their* documents, *their* tickets, *their* analyses. The moment someone watches a task that normally eats half a day get done in twenty minutes replaces any motivational speech. You can't lecture that moment into existence; people have to experience it.

## Train by role: not everyone needs the same thing

The second most common mistake after the wrong format: everyone gets the same content. Yet the needs differ drastically. A split that has proven itself:

**All employees (basics, about half a day plus a practice phase):** How do LLMs roughly work and why do they hallucinate? What data may go into which tool (data protection in practical terms, not legalese)? The handful of prompt patterns that deliver 80 percent of the everyday value - a precise task, context included, following up instead of giving up. And: critically checking what comes out. That's enough to avoid dangerous mistakes and capture real value - and it sensibly covers the literacy requirement of Article 4 of the EU AI Act along the way.

**Power users from the business units (2-3 days, spread out):** The people who build more complex analyses, create templates for their team, semi-automate recurring workflows. Content: advanced prompting, structured outputs, tool chains, first automations. This group is where your champions come from (more in a moment).

**Developers:** A field of its own - API usage, [agent development](/en/blog/building-ai-agents-practical-guide), evaluation. Belongs in the engineering track, not in broad training.

**Leadership (half a day, separate):** Not because they deserve better, but because their questions are different: What do I promise the board? How do I redesign processes? How do I lead a team that works with AI? And very practically: they have to use the tools themselves - leaders who preach adoption but have never opened an LLM undermine the whole effort.

## Reinforcement: what happens after the workshop decides everything

This is where lasting change separates from a pleasant memory. Three mechanisms that make the difference:

**1. Champions in every department.** One or two people per team who enjoy the topic, get extra training, and serve as the first point of contact. Important: this is a recognized role with a time budget (two to four hours a week is enough), not volunteer work on top. The question "who do you ask when you're stuck?" needs an answer that's closer than the IT hotline.

**2. Regular office hours.** An open slot every week or two where people bring their concrete cases. In the first weeks many come with "is this even possible?" questions; after two months the cases get more ambitious - that's the sign it's working.

**3. Shared prompts and templates.** A common place (wiki, shared library in the enterprise tool) holding working prompts for recurring tasks: the quote structure, the meeting summary, the complaint response. This dramatically lowers the entry barrier for the hesitant - they don't have to be creative, just apply.

## Dealing with fear and resistance

The topic that rarely appears in training vendors' proposals and hangs in the room at every real session: **"Is this going to replace my job?"**

My position, which I also state openly in companies: take the question seriously and answer it honestly. Honestly means: yes, job profiles will change - some tasks will disappear, others will emerge. Anyone claiming nothing will change for anyone is rightly not believed, and the training gets perceived as an imposition instead of an offer.

What actually works against the fear is mastery: people who command the tools experience themselves as shaping the change rather than being its objects. The difference in mood between a team that has AI "imposed on it" and one that has learned to make it work for them is palpable.

Two more things help: involve the works council early (not as a hurdle but as a partner - the worries that surface there are valuable information), and make bottom-up wins visible. The colleague who tells the story of cutting tender preparation from two days to half a day convinces more people than any management presentation.

## Measuring whether it works

Without measurement you won't know in six months whether the investment paid off. A small set is enough:

- **Active usage** of the official tools (weekly active users / licenses). Below 40 percent after three months means: fix the reinforcement.
- **Self-reported time savings** via a short survey every few months - imprecise, but meaningful as a trend.
- **Number of shared templates/prompts** and their usage - shows whether knowledge circulates through the company or evaporates in individual heads.
- **Use-case pipeline:** how many ideas keep coming from the business units? Trained workforces produce ideas; silence is an alarm signal.

## The first 90 days, concretely

A sequence that has proven itself several times:

- **Weeks 1-2:** Tool access and the one-page ground rule are in place. Leadership session first - the executives should know what's coming before everyone else does.
- **Weeks 3-6:** Basic workshops in groups of 10-15, half-day, on people's real tasks. Identify two champion candidates per group.
- **Weeks 5-8:** Champions deep-dive, office hours start, shared prompt library set up.
- **Weeks 8-12:** Second practice round in the teams ("what worked, what didn't?"), first metrics collected, use-case ideas gathered for the next stage.

After that it becomes routine: office hours stay, champions stay, and roughly once a year you need a refresh - today's tools won't be tomorrow's. Which is exactly why "train once" is wrong as a concept: you're not building a stock of knowledge, you're building the ability to keep learning.

## Frequently asked questions

**What exactly does the EU AI Act require in terms of AI literacy?**
Article 4 has obliged providers and deployers of AI systems since February 2025 to ensure their staff have sufficient AI literacy - appropriate to their role and context of use. There is no blanket certificate; documented, role-based training is the sensible way to comply. My advice: treat Article 4 not as a chore but as tailwind for something that's in your interest anyway.

**How much does decent AI training cost?**
As an order of magnitude for mid-sized companies: basic workshops run in the low four figures per day, plus participants' working time - the latter is the bigger item, and the reason exercises should use real tasks: then training time is already partly working time.

**Is it enough to train only the younger employees?**
The assumption underneath doesn't even hold: in my experience, age says little about AI affinity. Some of the best power users I've met in companies were over fifty - people with deep process knowledge who suddenly had a tool to leverage it. Process knowledge plus AI skills clearly beats youthful tool affinity.

**Should we rely on the tool vendors' ready-made training videos?**
As supplementary material, yes. As the main measure, no - vendor material naturally teaches the product, not your workflows, and the transfer to people's own tasks is once again left to the employees. But that transfer is precisely the hard part.

**How do we handle employees who don't want to?**
With patience and without coercion - but with a clear expectation. The baseline competence (what am I allowed to do, what comes out, how do I check it) is non-negotiable, if only for compliance reasons. Everything beyond that grows best through role models in one's own team. I have yet to see a team where the skepticism survived the first three colleagues visibly getting hours of their week back.
