> **Short answer:** AI transformation does not mean "we rolled out a tool." It means: provide the tools, build capability in your people, then redesign processes - in that order. The realistic path for most companies: a visible pilot with measurable value first (3 months), then broad skill-building (3-6 months), then genuinely rebuilding the first processes (from month 6). Companies that start with a license rollout or a governance committee instead end the year with licenses, a committee - and no change.

I'm writing this as a Professor of Applied AI at HdM Stuttgart. Before that I spent about 15 years building AI systems in industry - at Meta, the Bosch Center for AI, and Amazon Alexa - and I still work with companies on exactly the topic of this article.

Last updated: July 14, 2026.

## A word up front: I don't particularly like the word "transformation"

"Transformation" is one of those words consultancies love to put on slides because it sounds big and commits to nothing. I'm using it anyway, for a simple reason: for what is happening right now, "project" is simply the wrong word.

A project has an end. The change AI brings does not - at least not one anyone can see from here. The models improve every few months, the tooling keeps shifting, and what was a research topic last year (autonomous [AI agents](/en/blog/what-is-an-ai-agent), for instance) is running in production this year. A company that "completes" AI as a project will be starting over in eighteen months.

What I've seen across companies of every size over the past two years: the technology is almost never the problem. The problem is the organization around it - skills, processes, ownership, and above all: leadership expectations.

## What companies actually get out of AI - without the marketing numbers

Before the how, the why. In my experience the real value lands in four categories, in descending order of how easy they are to reach:

**1. Individual productivity.** Employees who have learned to work with LLMs complete writing, research, and analysis tasks measurably faster. 20 to 40 percent time savings on those task types is realistic - not on total working time; only vendor studies claim that. This value is the fastest to capture and still the most commonly wasted, because the training never happens.

**2. Throughput in text-heavy processes.** Wherever people currently read, sort, check, and route documents - support tickets, complaints, applications, invoices, contracts - LLM-based systems can multiply throughput. This is where the business cases that genuinely pay off live.

**3. Better, faster decisions.** If you can analyze unstructured data, you see things earlier: clusters in complaints, patterns in customer requests, gaps in documentation. Harder to quantify, but often the most durable effect.

**4. New offerings.** AI features in your own product, new services, new business models. Real - but for most companies not the starting point. It's what becomes possible once the first three categories are running.

The honest footnote: there are companies where little of this pays off right now. If your work is almost entirely physical and your processes involve little text, there are fewer levers today. That's not a failing, it's a sober assessment - and it is changing as agentic systems mature, more on that below.

## The three levels of AI transformation

In conversations with executives I always sort the topic into three levels. It helps, because each level has a different pace, different costs, and different owners.

### Level 1: Tools

Employees get access to good AI tools - typically an enterprise account with one of the major providers, with data protection sorted out. This is the easiest level, and it's mandatory: skip it and your people will use their private accounts anyway, just uncontrolled and with company data. Shadow AI exists in practically every company I know; the only question is whether you know about it.

Cost and duration: weeks, not months. Effect without level 2: close to zero.

### Level 2: People

The uncomfortable truth: a license rollout changes nothing. The difference between "we have Copilot" and "we work differently" is competence - and competence doesn't come from a one-hour talk. It comes from practicing on real tasks from your own daily work. I've written up what that looks like in a [separate article on AI training for employees](/en/blog/ai-training-for-employees).

This is where the transformation succeeds or fails. I've seen companies spend six figures on licenses whose active usage sat below 20 percent after three months. And I've seen teams permanently change how they work after two training days and four weeks of guided practice.

### Level 3: Processes

The advanced discipline - and the point where productivity gains turn into structural advantage. This is no longer about people doing their existing work faster. It's about recutting workflows: which steps does a system take over entirely? Where does a human review instead of create? Which steps disappear altogether?

This level is also where the [AI agents](/en/blog/agentic-ai-for-companies) everyone is talking about live. And here the rule holds: no level 3 without levels 1 and 2. A company whose employees don't understand AI cannot operate AI-driven processes - it won't even notice when something goes wrong.

## A realistic roadmap for the first twelve months

Every company is different, but the outline that has worked for me looks like this:

**Months 1-3: One pilot that proves something.** Pick *one* use case - narrowly scoped, measurable, human in the loop (I've described the criteria [here](/en/blog/ai-for-smbs)). Build it with a small team. The goal is not perfection but internal proof: "This works at *our* company, on *our* data." In parallel: sort out tool access and data protection so level 1 is in place.

**Months 3-6: Skills into the organization.** Now - not earlier - the training wave. The pilot gives you the stories and the internal advocates for it. Train by role, practice on real tasks, build champions in each department. This phase also produces your use-case pipeline: trained employees spot the opportunities themselves. The best ideas almost never come from the leadership team.

**Months 6-12: Rebuild processes, add structure.** The two or three best ideas from the pipeline become real initiatives - with an eval framework, monitoring, and proper operations. By now, at the latest, you need lightweight structures: who prioritizes use cases? Who approves tools? How do we handle the EU AI Act? The key word is *lightweight* - a one-page policy people actually follow beats a thirty-page one nobody reads.

And after that? Repeat. The transformation, as noted, is not a project.

## Where AI transformations fail

The five mistakes I see most often - roughly in order of damage done:

1. **Rolling out tools and hoping for miracles.** The classic. License costs run, usage flatlines, and a year later the verdict is "AI doesn't work for us." Nobody was ever taught how to use it.

2. **Starting with governance instead of value.** Some companies first found an AI board that spends months writing policies while nobody in the building gains practical experience. Policies without an experience base are theory - and usually so restrictive they smother every initiative. Pilot first; then write the rules you learned from it.

3. **Delegating the topic to IT.** AI transformation is an executive responsibility because it touches processes, roles, and priorities. IT is an indispensable partner - but if AI is "an IT topic," it becomes an infrastructure project and the business units watch from the sidelines.

4. **A lighthouse without daily practice.** An impressive showcase project for the board while 95 percent of the workforce has never touched AI. The reverse order is more stable: broad everyday competence first, ambitious flagship initiatives second.

5. **Measuring nothing.** If you track neither usage nor time saved nor quality, you cannot say after a year what any of it achieved - and you lose management commitment exactly when the bigger investments come up.

## What leadership specifically has to do

Finally, the part that generates the most note-taking in talks. AI transformation needs exactly four things from executives:

- **Use it yourself.** Leaders who preach AI adoption but have never opened an LLM are not taken seriously. Two or three real uses in your own daily work are enough - but you need them.
- **Free up time.** Building skills costs working hours. Stack training "on top" of full calendars and you get cancellations and no-shows. The math still works - the time saved afterwards is a multiple.
- **Declare tolerance for mistakes.** Things go wrong during the learning phase. If the first failed AI experiment gets publicly dissected, nobody experiments after that - at least not openly. The experimenting then migrates back into shadow AI.
- **Prioritize.** Not ten parallel initiatives, but two or three that actually get finished. AI transformations rarely die from too few ideas - almost always from too many at once.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long does an AI transformation take?**
The honest answer: it doesn't end. But the phase in which a company moves from "no experience" to "we noticeably work differently and have systems in production" takes 12 to 24 months of consistent effort. You should see the first measurable effects after 3 to 4 months - if not, something about the approach is off.

**Do we need a Chief AI Officer?**
Most mid-sized companies: no. What you need is one person with a mandate and time who drives the topic and has direct access to leadership. Whether they're called "CAIO," "AI coordinator," or just their name is secondary. Titles don't transform anything.

**Should we wait until the technology stabilizes?**
No - but not for the reason vendors give. It's not that a competitor will "pull away." It's that capability-building takes time and cannot be bought retroactively. A company that starts learning in 2026 can operate agentic systems in 2027. One that waits starts learning in 2027.

**How much budget should we plan?**
As an order of magnitude for a mid-sized company's first year: tool licenses depending on headcount, a pilot in the low to mid five-figure range, training in the low five figures. More important than the absolute number: budget for *people and learning*, not just software.

**What about the EU AI Act?**
Plan for it, don't be paralyzed by it. For the vast majority of internal applications the obligations are manageable (transparency, labeling, and AI literacy for staff under Article 4). High-risk use cases - hiring decisions, for example - need genuine care. The literacy obligation, by the way, is a good argument for taking the training question seriously anyway.
